# Memo intake

One question at a time. "I do not know" is recorded as a gap, never filled. Ask what this paper raises, not what staff papers usually raise. Never put a candidate answer inside the question.

## 1. Which paper, which memorandum, or a letter
1. What is this for: to record something that happened so it is in the file (memorandum for the record); to hand a senior the facts before a meeting (information paper); to carry the key points as a reminder (point paper); to get a decision (position/decision paper); to speak from at a meeting (talking paper); to write to another office or person inside your own activity (a memorandum, SECNAV M-5216.5 chapter 10); to put a mutual agreement between two commands in writing (memorandum of agreement or understanding); or to write to a company, an agency, or a person outside DoD (a business letter, chapter 11)? One purpose, one product.
2. If it is a memorandum, which one? The manual prints several and they are not interchangeable, and each has a condition in its own paragraph: the printed OPNAV form for a From-To between individuals and offices of the same activity (10-2.2); plain paper when there are multiple addressees, via addressees, or both (10-2.3); letterhead for more formality, or outside the activity when direct liaison is authorized and the matter is routine and "neither make[s] a commitment nor take[s] an official stand" (10-2.4). Which condition does this one meet?
3. Who reads it and what will they do with it? (For a decision paper: what exactly is the decision, as a yes or no the reader can sign? For a memorandum: is an approval or disapproval being asked of a single addressee, which is what 10-2.5 puts a decision block on?)

## 2. The facts
4. For an MFR: what happened, when, who was present by billet, what was said or decided, and what action is now underway and why? An MFR records; it does not argue.
5. For an information or point paper: what does the reader need to know walking in that they do not know now? What are the facts, with numbers and dates? What could go wrong in the meeting that the reader should see coming?
6. For a decision paper: what is the problem, why is a decision needed now, what has happened before, what do other sections or agencies hold (or "Not Applicable"), what position is recommended and on what reasoning, and what is the fallback?
7. For a From-To, plain-paper, or letterhead memorandum: who is it from and who is it to, by name, title, or code (10-2.2 allows all three); is anyone in the via line; what is the instruction or the information, and by what date must the addressee act? If it is going outside the activity, has direct liaison been authorized, and by whom?
8. For an MOA or MOU: which activities are agreeing; which of them is the senior; what is the purpose, the problem, and the scope; what is each party responsible for; and on what date does it take effect? Who is expected to sign last, and who then sends the copies? If a third activity is a party, it has a signature line too.
9. For a business letter: who is the addressee outside DoD, by courtesy title and full name, business title, business name, street address, and city, state, and ZIP+4; what are you asking for or answering; what earlier letter, call, or document is being referred to and on what date; and what is being enclosed? Is the letter going to a business in general with an attention line, or to a person?
10. Which references were actually used as sources? Five staff papers and memorandums cite them in the discussion. A business letter names none of them as references: 11-2.7, "Refer to previous communications and enclosures in the body of the letter only, without calling them references or enclosures." So for a letter the question is what earlier communication needs mentioning in the body, by date, in plain words.

## 3. Form
11. Identification symbols, which differ by kind and are not guessed. For an MFR the code and date as Appendix D prints them. For a point, decision, talking, or information paper the originator code and the date (the day of dispatch for an information paper). For a From-To or plain-paper memorandum, the date and nothing else unless local practice calls for more (10-2.2 and 10-2.3.a). For a letterhead memorandum, figure 10-4 shows an SSIC, a serial with "Memo" in it, and the date; paragraph 10-2.4 is silent, so ask what the command uses. For an MOA or MOU, each activity's own short title, SSIC, serial, and date, one column each. For a business letter, the SSIC, the originator's code, and the date in the civilian format 11-2.1.c sets: month spelled out, day, comma, full year.
12. Signature, which also differs by kind and is the part most often carried over from the wrong format. MFR: the signer's name, billet, and grade and service as Appendix D prints them. From-To: the name alone, because figure 10-2 paragraph 6 says "The writer signs his or her name without the organizational titles." Letterhead: a full signature line is not required, because the From line identifies the signer (10-2.4). MOA or MOU: the senior official's line at the right, a third cosigner's in the middle, each preceded by overscoring, and the senior activity signs after the junior (10-2.6.d). Business letter: "Sincerely," then, on the fourth line below it at the centre of the page, the name in capitals, the grade spelled out, the functional title, and an authority line unless the letter is routine and neither commits nor takes an official stand (11-2.8 and 11-2.9). Identity from the rules file; nothing guessed.
13. Classification: unclassified papers carry no markings; the publication's samples show where (U) portion marks go if the paper is ever classified. Chapters 10 and 11 do not address classification, so a classified memorandum or letter is a question for the command. Confirm unclassified.
14. Length: a point or information paper is one page (two at most for an information or decision paper, by the publication). What must be cut or moved to a tab to hold that? Chapters 10 and 11 set no page limit on a memorandum or a business letter; 11-2.15 only says how to number a second page.

## 4. Correlate to the standard
15. Hold the draft against the paragraph or appendix for that product: every required part present in the publication's order and heading words; the recommendation, if there is one, one the reader can approve or disapprove with a signature; nothing the reader would have to look up. For a business letter, walk the four traps in `references/standard.md`, "The four things that catch a Marine out", one at a time.

## 5. The reader
16. How does this staff want papers written (paragraphs or ticks; who approves; whether the commander wants the recommendation first)? Which memorandum form does the activity actually stock, since 10-2.2 and figure 10-2 give different OPNAV numbers for the same two forms? Record in `Overrides/memo.md`.

## The quantification ladder
| Rung | Question |
|---|---|
| Action | What happened, or what is asked? |
| Scope | When, for how many, over what period? |
| Result | What was decided or found, in a number where there is one? |
| Comparison | Against the plan, the standard, or last time? |
| Consequence | What does the reader now need to do, and by when? |
Stop at the rung the writer can defend from the record.
