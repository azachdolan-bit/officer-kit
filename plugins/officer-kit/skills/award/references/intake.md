# Award intake: the questions, in the order a good XO asks them

The point of the intake is three correlations, in this order: what the Marine actually did, quantified; whether that reaches the manual's criteria for the level requested; and whether the write up says exactly that and nothing more. Fluff is a failure, not a style choice.

Ask one question at a time. Accept "I do not know" and move on; a gap is reported, never filled.

## 1. Frame

1. Which award, and who approves it at your command? (Sets the criteria, the SOA length and format, and the reader.)
2. Specific achievement or sustained performance? Impact award, transfer, retirement, or end of tour? (The order says a routine end of tour recommendation has no place; if that is what this is, ask question 4 harder.)
3. What billet, over what dates, and was the billet above the Marine's grade? (A Sergeant in an SNCO billet is a fact the board weighs; say it once, in the first paragraph.)

## 2. What actually happened

4. What is the single hardest or most consequential thing this Marine did in the period? One sentence. (This leads the SOA and the citation.)
5. What was the state before and after, in a number a reader could check? (T-4 to T-1; 78 to 96 percent; a first for the battalion.)
6. What broke, or was missing, and what did the Marine do about it? (The provider cancelled two weeks out; the tracking system did not show augments.)
7. How many people, events, hours, dollars, or items, over what period? For each achievement. (Every strong sentence in an approved citation has one.)
8. Who else saw it, and is there a record? (A readiness rating, a roster count, a selection, a named exercise.)
9. What did the unit get that it would not have had? (E-5 and below attended free; 183 Marines returned able to lead the next cycle.)
10. Anything outside the billet worth one paragraph? (Volunteer hours with counts and organizations. One paragraph, never the lead.)

## 3. Correlate to the manual

11. Read the criteria for the level requested from the user's copy of SECNAV M-1650.1 chapter 2 (and the order's criteria guide in the extract). Hold the answers to 4 through 9 against them. Say one of three things:
    - The facts reach the level as the manual describes it. Proceed.
    - The facts reach a lower level. Say which, quote the criterion that separates them, and offer to write to that level or to keep the request with the gap stated.
    - The facts do not reach a personal decoration. Say so and offer a Certificate of Commendation or a letter of appreciation.
    The tool never promises a board's answer. It makes sure the argument for the level is on the page and that the recommender knows the gap if there is one.

## 4. Correlate to reality

12. Before drafting, read the answers back as a fact list: each item with its number, period, and source. The user confirms or corrects. Nothing outside the confirmed list goes in.
13. After drafting, the citation is checked against the SOA mechanically: every number in the citation appears in the SOA exactly; every claim in the citation has a sentence in the SOA behind it.

## 5. The reader

14. How does the approval authority read these, if the user knows? (Result first, or chronology; bullets or paragraphs; anything they strike on sight.) Record it as a reader profile in the user's folder for next time.

## The quantification ladder

For any vague note ("did a great job on the range"), climb:

| Rung | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Action | What did the Marine do? | ran the platoon's rifle qualification |
| Scope | For how many, how often, how long? | 42 Marines over two ranges in five days |
| Result | What changed, in a number? | qualification from 78 to 96 percent |
| Comparison | Against what? | the highest in the battalion for the cycle; up from last cycle's 81 |
| Consequence | What did the unit get? | every Marine deployable on the qualification line for the MEU work up |

Stop at the rung the recommender can defend. A sentence that stops at Scope is honest; a sentence that invents a Comparison is not.
