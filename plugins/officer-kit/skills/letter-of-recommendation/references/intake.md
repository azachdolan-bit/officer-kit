# Letter of recommendation intake

One question at a time. "I do not know" is recorded, not filled.

## The reader

1. What is this for, and who decides? (MECEP, ECP, a school seat, recruiting or drill instructor screening, a civilian job or university.) The program's instruction, if the user has it, names what the board weighs; read it and list those items before asking anything else.
2. When is it due, to whom, and in what form (naval letter, a form field with a character limit, an email)?
3. Is there required content? Some programs require the writer to address specific qualities or to state a ranking. List them.

## Standing

4. Who is the writer to the Marine: commanding officer, officer in charge, platoon commander, staff NCO, peer? Over what period, in what billets? Direct daily observation, or through a chain?
5. If the user is drafting for someone else to sign: what did that person actually see? A letter is signed by the person who observed; the tool drafts only what the signer can stand behind.

## The ranking statement

6. Where does this Marine stand among the Marines of that grade the writer has served with, and how many is that, over what years? ("The best sergeant of the roughly forty I have led in six years." "In the top three of my twelve platoon sergeants.") If the writer will not rank, the letter says "recommended" without a ranking and the tool says why that is weaker.

The ranking statement is the sentence the board is deciding on. A weak ranking ("among the best") without a denominator reads as reluctance. A specific ranking from someone with standing is the strongest thing a letter can carry.

## Specifics

7. What billets did the Marine hold under the writer, and were any above grade? For how long?
8. Two to four facts with numbers that bear on what the reader is deciding: Marines led, personnel trained, dollars accounted for, a readiness change, a selection, a school completed in what standing. Each with its period and a source.
9. If the program is about leadership potential or academic potential, what has the writer seen that shows it, in a number where possible (off duty credits completed while deployed; a section run for four months without an SNCO)?

## The scene

10. One thing the writer watched the Marine do that showed what the letter claims. When, what happened, what the Marine did, what came of it. One paragraph, dated, plain.

## The close

11. What does the writer expect the Marine to do if selected? What will the Marine do if not selected (a fact, not a threat: "will reapply next cycle")?
12. Who is the point of contact and how are they reached?

## What stays out

Anything medical, family, financial, disciplinary, or from an investigation, even framed as something overcome. The scanner fails the draft on it. Identifiers beyond grade and name.
